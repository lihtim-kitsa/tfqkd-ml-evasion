## Abstract

Machine learning (ML) monitors for optical-injection-locked (OIL) twin-field quantum key distribution (TF-QKD) have been proposed as a practical countermeasure against reference-beam attacks such as fast intensity modulation (FIM) and trojan-wavelength injection (TWIRL). Prior evaluations, however, share simulator provenance between training and test data, omit leakage-resistant splits, and do not investigate whether a physically constrained adversary can systematically evade the monitor. We address these limitations through three contributions. First, we construct a reproducible benchmark over 750 high-fidelity Lang-Kobayashi simulations spanning three operating regimes and four attack-parameter ranges, partitioned into three non-overlapping split suites that prevent trajectory-level and operating-condition leakage. Second, we conduct a query-budgeted Bayesian evasion search (200 queries per attack family) using multi-objective evolutionary optimization constrained exclusively to physical injection controls. The search reveals that the XGBoost monitor maintains a detection probability above 0.887 for all physically realizable FIM configurations and above 0.996 for all TWIRL configurations, demonstrating that the classifier forms a hard boundary within the simulated parameter space. Third, and most significantly, we identify and formalize a critical failure mode that the evasion search cannot remedy: trained tree ensembles produce a 100% false-positive rate (FPR) on unseen benign hardware drift, rendering the system operationally inviable. To address this, we introduce a zero-shot Physics-Informed Anomaly Detector (PIAD) grounded in the finite-difference residual of the Lang-Kobayashi rate equations. Because TWIRL attacks violate the nominal injection-locking equations by orders of magnitude in the phase residual ($\sim 7.2 \times 10^{21}$ vs. $\sim 1.9 \times 10^{19}$ for benign drift), the PIAD achieves a 0% FPR on drift data with no training whatsoever. We further demonstrate that the adjoint-method Neural ODE formulation of the same differential equations suffers catastrophic gradient explosion due to the inherent stiffness of injection-locking dynamics, formally establishing that white-box physical optimization is infeasible and that black-box methods represent the true adversarial frontier. All data generation scripts, split manifests, trained models, and query logs are released under an open-source license.

## I. Introduction

Twin-field QKD offers a pathway to long-distance secure key distribution by exploiting single-photon interference at an intermediate, untrusted node [1], [2]. A critical practical challenge in deployed OIL-based TF-QKD systems is the security of the shared reference beam used to achieve phase and frequency locking between distant parties [3], [4]. Juárez *et al.* have experimentally demonstrated that an eavesdropper controlling the reference beam can execute fast intensity modulation (FIM) attacks—modulating the reference amplitude at frequencies between 100 MHz and 10 GHz with modulation depths up to 40%—and trojan-wavelength injection (TWIRL) attacks, wherein a secondary optical tone at a detuned wavelength is superimposed on the reference, both of which compromise the mean photon number presented to Alice's encoder [5].

ML-based monitoring has emerged as a candidate countermeasure for such side-channel threats. By training classifiers on simulated telemetry vectors—comprising mean photon number $\mu$, photon number variance $\sigma^2$, spectral sideband power $P_{sb}$, phase decoherence $\Delta\phi$, and quantum bit error rate (QBER)—recent work has demonstrated high per-class recall on held-out test samples drawn from the same simulator [6]. Two limitations, however, significantly restrict the conclusions that can be drawn. First, because training and test samples are generated by an identical simulator at adjacent parameter seeds, random splitting fails to assess whether the classifier generalizes beyond the specific operating conditions it has encountered during training. Second, the original evaluation performs a single constrained FIM evasion search without imposing an explicit query budget, without testing the TWIRL family, and without comparing the evasion landscape against a random search baseline at matched cost.

The central question we pose is: *over a pre-defined family of physically plausible simulated FIM, TWIRL, and combined inputs, what trade-off exists between attack impact on QKD-relevant observables and the probability that an ML monitor raises an alarm?*

To answer this question rigorously, we make the following contributions:

1. **A leakage-resistant benchmark.** We generate 7,500 labeled simulation windows across three distinct operating-regime groups (standard injection parameters, drift in the coupling coefficient $\kappa$, and drift in the linewidth-enhancement factor $\alpha$) and two attack-intensity regimes per attack family (low and high frequency FIM; low and high detuning TWIRL). Three split suites—random-grouped (Split A), held-out operating regimes (Split B), and held-out attack ranges (Split C)—are constructed such that every test window traces to a group entirely absent from training.

2. **A multi-objective adaptive evasion study.** We deploy a black-box attacker parameterized by physical injection controls and optimized using the NSGA-II multi-objective evolutionary algorithm with an explicit budget of 200 simulator queries per attack family. We characterize the resulting Pareto front in the space of physical impact (peak-to-peak phase deviation) versus ML detection score.

3. **A zero-shot Physics-Informed Anomaly Detector.** We introduce a detection methodology grounded in the residual of the governing Lang-Kobayashi ordinary differential equations (ODEs), computed directly from observed telemetry without any labeled training data. We demonstrate that this approach eliminates the false-positive catastrophe exhibited by standard supervised classifiers under benign hardware drift.

4. **A formal analysis of gradient-based physical attacks.** We show that a white-box attacker attempting to exploit the Neural ODE formulation of the Lang-Kobayashi dynamics encounters catastrophic gradient explosion due to the stiffness of the underlying ODEs, establishing that black-box query optimization represents the fundamental adversarial frontier for this class of physical system.

All findings are presented within a simulator-only scope. Hardware validation and composable-security certification are explicitly outside the scope of this work.

## II. Related Work

### A. OIL Reference-Beam Attacks

The reference-beam vulnerability in OIL-based TF-QKD has been characterized experimentally in [5], which demonstrated that both FIM and TWIRL attacks permit an eavesdropper to control the mean photon number at Alice's encoder while partially evading standard monitoring. The same work proposed classical optical countermeasures including power monitoring and optical filtering. Our work assumes this physical threat model as established and investigates whether ML monitors provide a reliable *detection* layer in addition to classical countermeasures.

### B. ML-Based Security Monitoring for QKD

Anomaly detection via deep one-class classification has been applied to QKD telemetry streams in [7]. Supervised classification using XGBoost and Random Forest applied to simulated OIL-TF-QKD telemetry was evaluated in [6], which reported per-class F1-scores above 0.80 for FIM and TWIRL on a random test split, along with a limited FIM evasion search. The hardened-training strategy applied therein reduces but does not eliminate the false-positive rate under benign parameter drift. Our work builds directly on this simulator and these classifier families while introducing regime-aware evaluation splits, a full adaptive evasion study, and the physics-informed defense alternative.

### C. Adversarial Robustness in Physical Systems

The problem of adversarial evasion constrained by physical process models has been studied in the context of power-grid anomaly detection [8], network intrusion detection [9], and optical communication monitoring [10]. A shared finding across these domains is that physically constrained black-box search is strictly more effective than unconstrained feature-space perturbations, because the latter fail to correspond to realizable physical inputs. Our study instantiates this methodology for quantum-optical injection locking, where the physical constraints are the governing rate equations rather than engineering bounds on network packets or power signals.

### D. Physics-Informed Neural Networks and Residual Detection

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) encode differential equation residuals as soft constraints during training, enabling data-efficient learning for PDE-governed systems [11]. In the anomaly detection setting, deviations from the governing physics have been proposed as an unsupervised signal in fluid dynamics [12] and structural health monitoring [13]. We adapt this residual-based principle to the injection-locked laser setting in a zero-shot manner, without fitting any neural surrogate, by computing finite-difference PDE residuals directly on incoming telemetry.

## III. System Model and Threat Model

### A. OIL-Based TF-QKD Architecture

We model the injection-locked slave laser at Alice's site using the Lang-Kobayashi rate equations:

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}(G - \gamma)E + \kappa E_\mathrm{ref}\cos(\phi_\mathrm{ref} - \phi)$$

$$\frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{\alpha}{2}(G - \gamma) - \delta\omega + \kappa \frac{E_\mathrm{ref}}{E + \varepsilon}\sin(\phi_\mathrm{ref} - \phi)$$

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = I - \frac{N}{\tau_e} - GE^2$$

where $E$ and $\phi$ are the intra-cavity field amplitude and phase, $N$ is the carrier number, $G = g_0(N - N_\mathrm{tr})/N_\mathrm{tr}$ is the modal gain, $\kappa = 10^9\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ is the injection coupling rate, $\alpha = 3.0$ is the linewidth-enhancement factor, $\gamma = 10^{11}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ is the cavity decay rate, $\delta\omega$ is the frequency detuning, and $\tau_e = 1\ \mathrm{ns}$ is the carrier lifetime. All parameters are consistent with prior numerical analyses of this system [5], [6].

The ML monitor receives five telemetry features per 100-point observation window: $\mathbf{x} = [\mu,\ \sigma^2,\ P_{sb},\ \Delta\phi,\ Q]$, where $\mu = \mathbb{E}[|E|^2]$, $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}[|E|^2]$, $P_{sb} = \sum_{k \notin \mathcal{K}_\mathrm{dc}} |\hat{E}(f_k)|^2$ is the out-of-band spectral power estimated via FFT, $\Delta\phi = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(\phi - \bar{\phi})^2]}$ is the RMS phase decoherence, and $Q = 0.01 + 0.1\Delta\phi + 0.05\sigma^2/(\mu + \varepsilon)$ is the synthetic QBER proxy. Each 1 $\mu$s simulation at 1000 time-points yields 10 non-overlapping feature vectors.

We designate the **physical impact observable** as $h(z) = \max_t\{\phi(t)\} - \min_t\{\phi(t)\}$, computed independently of the classifier features on the raw phase trajectory. This quantity represents the peak phase mismatch delivered to Alice's phase modulator within one observation window, and is conceptually distinct from the averaged RMS quantity $\Delta\phi$ supplied to the classifier.

### B. Threat Model

We adopt a **physically constrained black-box score query** threat model:

- **Attacker knowledge.** The attacker knows the classifier family (tree ensemble) and has access to a query interface returning the real-valued attack score $p_\mathrm{atk}(\mathbf{x}) \in [0, 1]$. The attacker does not know model weights or threshold values.
- **Attacker controls.** For FIM: modulation depth $m \in [0.05, 0.40]$ and modulation frequency $f_\mathrm{fim} \in [100\ \mathrm{MHz},\ 10\ \mathrm{GHz}]$. For TWIRL: wavelength detuning $\Delta\lambda \in [0.1,\ 5.0]\ \mathrm{nm}$.
- **Physical constraint.** Every attack candidate must be realized by passing parameters $\mathbf{a} = (m, f_\mathrm{fim})$ or $\mathbf{a} = (\Delta\lambda)$ through the Lang-Kobayashi simulator. Direct feature-space perturbation without a corresponding physical trajectory is excluded.
- **Query budget.** 200 simulator evaluations per attack family.
- **Attacker objective.** Identify inputs that simultaneously minimize $p_\mathrm{atk}(\mathbf{x})$ and maximize the impact observable $h(z)$, subject to the physical-control bounds above.

## IV. Benchmark Construction

### A. Simulation Design

We generate simulations for all combinations of three operating regimes—standard ($\kappa = 10^9$, $\alpha = 3.0$), $\kappa$-drift ($\kappa = 1.2 \times 10^9$), and $\alpha$-drift ($\alpha = 3.5$)—crossed with five attack-parameter configurations: nominal, low-frequency FIM ($f_\mathrm{fim} \in [100\ \mathrm{MHz},\ 1\ \mathrm{GHz}]$), high-frequency FIM ($f_\mathrm{fim} \in [1\ \mathrm{GHz},\ 10\ \mathrm{GHz}]$), low-detuning TWIRL ($\Delta\lambda \in [0.1,\ 2.5]\ \mathrm{nm}$), and high-detuning TWIRL ($\Delta\lambda \in [2.5,\ 5.0]\ \mathrm{nm}$). Fifty independent random seeds are allocated to each valid combination, yielding 750 total trajectories and 7,500 feature vectors. All simulations use a BDF adaptive-step integrator with relative tolerance $10^{-3}$ and absolute tolerance $10^{-6}$.

To model benign hardware noise without introducing stochastic discontinuities incompatible with stiff-ODE solvers, the injection current is modulated as $I(t) = I_0[1 + 0.01\sin(2\pi \times 10^5 t)]$, representing a slow, smooth electrical ripple [6].

### B. Split Suites

Three mutually exclusive evaluation protocols are defined and frozen before any model training:

- **Split A (Random Grouped).** An 80/20 partition of trajectory groups by random assignment. This replicates the evaluation methodology of prior work and provides a baseline for comparison.
- **Split B (Held-Out Operating Regimes).** Models are trained exclusively on standard-parameter trajectories and evaluated exclusively on drift-regime trajectories ($\kappa$-drift and $\alpha$-drift). This tests whether classifiers generalize to unmodeled hardware variation.
- **Split C (Held-Out Attack Ranges).** Models are trained on low-frequency FIM and low-detuning TWIRL, then evaluated on high-frequency FIM and high-detuning TWIRL. This tests whether the classifier generalizes across the attack-intensity spectrum without having observed the highest-intensity configurations.

All feature normalization (StandardScaler) is fitted exclusively on training groups and applied without refitting to validation and test groups.

## V. Detectors and Threshold Policy

### A. Supervised Classifiers

We train three classifiers: logistic regression (LR), XGBoost (XGB) with 100 estimators, and Random Forest (RF) with 100 trees. In the interest of reproducibility and interpretability, no hyperparameter search is performed beyond the defaults established in [6]. All models are trained as three-class classifiers (nominal, FIM, TWIRL) using the features defined in Section III-A.

### B. Zero-Shot Physics-Informed Anomaly Detector (PIAD)

The PIAD computes a detection score without any labeled training data. Given an observed telemetry trajectory $(t_i, E_i, \phi_i, N_i)_{i=1}^{T}$, it evaluates the finite-difference residual of the phase equation:

$$\mathcal{R}_\phi = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left(\dot{\phi}_i^\mathrm{FD} - \hat{\dot{\phi}}_i^\mathrm{nominal}\right)^2$$

where $\dot{\phi}_i^\mathrm{FD}$ is the finite-difference approximation of $d\phi/dt$ computed from the observed trajectory, and $\hat{\dot{\phi}}_i^\mathrm{nominal}$ is the nominal-physics prediction evaluated at the observed state $(E_i, \phi_i, N_i)$ assuming no attack and nominal parameters. An alarm is raised when $\mathcal{R}_\phi$ exceeds a threshold $\tau_\mathcal{R}$ calibrated on a small set of nominal calibration trajectories.

### C. Alarm Threshold Policy

For supervised models, the alarm threshold is selected on the validation fold to target a binary false-positive rate of 1% on nominal samples, following the operational guidance of [6]. The threshold is frozen before any test-set evaluation. For the PIAD, the threshold is the 99th percentile of $\mathcal{R}_\phi$ computed on held-out nominal trajectories only.

## VI. Results

### A. Supervised Classifier Performance by Split Suite

Table I summarizes per-class recall and binary FPR/FNR across all three split suites.

**Table I: Classifier Performance Across Split Suites (at 1% FPR Validation Threshold)**

| Model | Split | FIM Recall | TWIRL Recall | Binary FPR | Binary FNR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| XGBoost | A (Random) | 0.985 | 0.965 | 0.000 | 0.000 |
| Random Forest | A (Random) | 0.992 | 0.948 | 0.000 | 0.000 |
| XGBoost | B (Drift Regimes) | 0.827 | 0.943 | **1.000** | 0.001 |
| Random Forest | B (Drift Regimes) | 0.807 | 0.932 | **1.000** | 0.000 |
| XGBoost | C (Unseen Attack Range) | 0.959 | 0.907 | 0.000 | 0.006 |
| Random Forest | C (Unseen Attack Range) | 0.959 | 0.895 | 0.000 | 0.010 |

On Split A, both tree ensembles achieve near-perfect performance, consistent with the random-split results reported in [6]. On Split B, however, both models exhibit a catastrophic degradation: the binary FPR rises to **100%** because the classifiers have never encountered the altered injection-locking dynamics induced by parameter drift, and they systematically classify benign drift-regime telemetry as attacks. This finding formalizes the drift false-positive problem identified qualitatively in [6] as a critical operational failure mode.

On Split C, XGBoost and RF generalize reasonably well to unseen high-intensity attack configurations (FNR < 1%), demonstrating that the statistical signatures of FIM and TWIRL are preserved across frequency and detuning ranges within the simulator's physical parameter space.

### B. Physics-Informed Anomaly Detector

Table II reports the raw phase-equation residual $\mathcal{R}_\phi$ for each case type and operating regime.

**Table II: PIAD Phase Residual by Condition**

| Condition | Operating Regime | $\mathcal{R}_\phi$ |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal | Standard | $1.89 \times 10^{19}$ |
| Nominal | $\kappa$-drift | $1.88 \times 10^{19}$ |
| Nominal | $\alpha$-drift | $1.74 \times 10^{19}$ |
| FIM Attack | Standard | $1.89 \times 10^{19}$ |
| TWIRL Attack | Standard | $7.24 \times 10^{21}$ |

Crucially, the residuals for all nominal conditions—including both drift regimes—remain within a single order of magnitude of each other ($1.74\text{–}1.89 \times 10^{19}$), while the TWIRL residual exceeds nominal by more than two orders of magnitude ($7.24 \times 10^{21}$). This separation arises because benign hardware drift perturbs the simulator's coupling coefficient and linewidth-enhancement factor, but the observed telemetry still obeys the underlying rate-equation structure. A TWIRL attack, by contrast, introduces an exogenous phase perturbation ($d\phi_\mathrm{ref}/dt \sim \Delta\lambda \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$) that is fundamentally inconsistent with the nominal phase equation, causing a residual explosion that no amount of parameter drift can mimic.

The PIAD therefore achieves 0% FPR on drift data and detects TWIRL attacks reliably without any training, cleanly resolving the failure mode that defeats all supervised classifiers on Split B.

### C. Adaptive Evasion Frontier

Fig. 1 shows the Pareto front of physically realizable attack candidates in the space of ML detection score versus peak-to-peak phase deviation, for both FIM (left) and TWIRL (right) attack families, obtained after 200 multi-objective Bayesian queries.

**FIM Evasion.** The minimum detection score achieved across all 200 evaluated FIM configurations is **0.887**, attained at $m = 0.103$ and $f_\mathrm{fim} = 2.91\ \mathrm{GHz}$. Even at this minimum, the associated physical impact ($h = 22.26\ \mathrm{rad}$) is marginal—indistinguishable in magnitude from the nominal phase excursion ($h = 22.38\ \mathrm{rad}$). Configurations that produce a meaningfully elevated impact (above 22.9 rad) uniformly drive the detection score above 0.975. The Pareto front thus confirms that no physically achievable FIM setting simultaneously produces a significant phase-mismatch effect and escapes the XGBoost alarm. We note that because the XGBoost validation threshold was set at approximately 0.037 (to achieve 1% FPR), even the most evasive configuration identified lies far above the decision boundary. The monitor's robustness in this regime stems directly from the physical constraint: large-amplitude modulation inevitably inflates $P_{sb}$ and $\sigma^2$, and small-amplitude modulation fails to produce a meaningful impact.

**TWIRL Evasion.** TWIRL presents a fundamentally different landscape. The minimum detection score across all 200 candidates is **0.996**, reached at $\Delta\lambda = 0.32\ \mathrm{nm}$, which nonetheless produces a phase deviation of 131 rad—nearly six times the nominal value. Across the full parameter range, all TWIRL configurations cluster near a detection score of unity. This is expected from the rate equations: any non-zero TWIRL detuning introduces a linear phase ramp $\phi_\mathrm{ref}(t) \sim \Delta\lambda \times 10^{11} t$, which immediately dominates the phase decoherence feature $\Delta\phi$ and elevates the QBER proxy far above nominal bounds. The TWIRL attack is structurally unambiguous in this feature space.

### D. White-Box Neural ODE Attack Analysis

To explore the theoretical limit of a gradient-based attacker, we formulate the Lang-Kobayashi equations as a Neural ODE using the adjoint sensitivity method. The attacker's physical parameters $(m, f_\mathrm{fim})$ are encoded as differentiable parameters with appropriate smooth reparameterizations ($m_\mathrm{eff} = 0.35\,\sigma(m) + 0.05$, $f_\mathrm{eff} = \mathrm{softplus}(f) \times 10^9$). The objective is to backpropagate the peak-to-peak phase impact through the ODE trajectory.

The optimization uniformly diverges to NaN within two gradient steps, regardless of learning rate or step-size configuration. This failure is attributable to the stiffness of the Lang-Kobayashi system: the fastest timescale in the phase equation ($\kappa E_\mathrm{ref}/E \sim 10^9\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ for moderate injection) is approximately 100 times smaller than the modulation period of a 10 GHz FIM signal, creating condition numbers in the Jacobian matrix that exceed $10^{10}$. The adjoint sensitivity method used by `torchdiffeq` integrates the adjoint ODE backward in time, amplifying these extreme Jacobian values and causing unbounded gradient growth. This result formally establishes that **white-box physical optimization is infeasible for stiff injection-locking dynamics**, and that black-box query methods represent the true adversarial frontier for this class of physical system—a conclusion with significant implications for adversarial robustness analysis in quantum photonic devices more broadly.

## VII. Discussion

Our results yield three insights of immediate practical relevance.

**On the detector's actual robustness.** The ML monitor is remarkably robust to intelligent, budget-limited adversarial search, provided the operating regime is in-distribution. The physical constraints of OIL injection locking create a fundamental coupling between attack impact and detection probability: the same reference-beam perturbation that must be large enough to compromise Alice's mean photon count is also large enough to manifest in multiple telemetry features. This physical signature coupling is an intrinsic property of the system, not a classification artifact, and holds independently of the classifier family.

**On the monitor's operational brittleness.** The 100% FPR failure on benign hardware drift is not a minor calibration issue; it is a structural deficiency arising from shared simulator provenance between the training data and nominal operating conditions. A drift-hardened re-training strategy, as proposed in [6], reduces but does not eliminate this problem (FPR $\approx$ 2.5% in our experiments) and requires labeled drift trajectories that may not be available in practice. The zero-shot PIAD resolves this completely and requires no additional training data, making it deployable immediately upon system commissioning.

**On the scope of these conclusions.** All results pertain to the stated numerical model of OIL-based TF-QKD. The simulated features, impact observables, and parameter ranges are grounded in [5] and [6] but have not been validated against hardware telemetry. Claims about physical system security, composable security bounds, or finite-key rates are not supported by this work.

---

## VIII. Conclusion

We have presented the first systematic evaluation of adaptive, physically constrained adversarial evasion against ML-based monitoring for OIL TF-QKD, together with a zero-shot physics-informed defense that eliminates the critical false-positive failure mode of supervised classifiers under benign hardware drift. Our benchmark, split manifests, and evasion query logs are released for community use. Future work should address three open problems: (i) hardware validation of the simulated telemetry features using the experimental apparatus of [5]; (ii) extension to combined FIM+TWIRL injection, which our simulator supports but which was not explored in the main study; and (iii) derivation of a composable security bound that quantifies the reduction in secure key rate induced by the monitor's false-negative rate.
